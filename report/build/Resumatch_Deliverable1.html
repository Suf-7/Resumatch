1Introduction

Resumatch is a web application that matches resumes and job postings in both directions. A job seeker uploads a CV and receives the three best-matching open job postings they can apply to. Each comes with a direct apply link, an ATS-style score for that specific posting, the matched and missing skills, and suggested rewrites of weak resume bullets. Recruiters upload a job description together with a batch of CVs and receive a ranked, explained shortlist. The matching, ranking, and skill-extraction components are models we train or fine-tune ourselves; external LLM APIs are used only in supporting roles (pre-labelling, synthetic data generation, and natural-language explanations).

This document covers Phase 1 (Problem Definition) and Phase 2 (Data Acquisition & Preprocessing). Unlike a plan, every number reported in Phase 2 was produced by the scripts in the project repository (data_scripts/) and is regenerated automatically into this report from report/stats/*.json.

Phase 1: Problem Definition

2Problem Statement

Hiring is a two-sided matching problem that both sides handle poorly.

For candidates.  Many applicants, especially fresh graduates, write one generic CV and send it everywhere, and rarely learn why they were not shortlisted. Applications are first screened by Applicant Tracking Systems (ATS) and by recruiters who spend very little time per CV, so a strong candidate can be filtered out because the resume does not surface the skills a posting asks for, or describes them in different words (“built REST services” versus “API development”).

For recruiters.  A single posting can attract far more applications than can be read carefully. Keyword filters are the usual remedy, but they reward keyword stuffing and miss capable candidates who phrase their experience differently.

The gap.  Existing tools are mostly either (a) simple keyword counters or (b) paid, closed products that give a score without explaining it. There is little transparent, evaluable tooling that (i) ranks in both directions, (ii) explains the specific missing skills, and (iii) is audited for bias against attributes such as name, gender, or university. Our own data (Section 9) confirms the vocabulary problem: 976 of the 2,278 distinct skills requested in postings never appear in any resume, and 22 of the 100 most requested skills appear in fewer than 1% of resumes.

3Target Audience

  • Primary: students and fresh graduates applying for internships and entry-level roles in Pakistan, who need actionable feedback on how well their CV fits a role.
  • Secondary: early-career professionals switching roles or domains who need to see their skill gaps.
  • Secondary: recruiters and HR staff at small companies and startups without an enterprise ATS, who need a fast, explainable shortlist.
  • Academic: the bias-audit component addresses an open research concern in automated hiring tools.

4AI Justification

A rule-based system cannot solve this problem well, for four reasons:

  1. Semantic variation. The same skill appears in many surface forms (“JS”, “JavaScript”, “Node.js”; “ML”, “machine learning”). ESCO alone lists 99,192 alternative labels for 13,896 skills; a keyword system needs an exhaustive and constantly outdated synonym list. Sentence embeddings place semantically similar text close together without hand-written rules.
  2. Context and level. “Used Python once in a course” and “led a Python team for three years” share a keyword but signal very different fit. A learned model can use context; a keyword counter cannot.
  3. Skill extraction from unstructured text. CVs and postings have no fixed format. Named-entity recognition (NER) models learn to tag skills from examples instead of relying on brittle regular expressions. Our dictionary baseline (Section 8) needed a hand-built list of ambiguous words (“it”, “current”, “balance”, “chef”) to avoid false matches, which illustrates exactly this brittleness.
  4. Ranking is a learned preference. A “good match” combines many weak signals (skills, seniority, domain, experience). A trained ranker can learn their weights from labelled pairs; hand-set weights cannot be validated.

Evidence from our data. For every resume we mined hard negatives: postings from a different occupation that are lexically close to the resume. They are as similar to the resume as true same-occupation positives (mean TF-IDF cosine 0.13 vs. 0.12). Because hard negatives are mined with TF-IDF, that baseline is disadvantaged by construction (AUC 0.40). The independent, off-the-shelf MiniLM sentence encoder does only slightly better, at AUC 0.54, barely above chance. Telling the right role from a similar-sounding wrong one therefore needs encoders fine-tuned on our pairs, which is the Phase 3 plan. Our keyword-style baseline ATS score (Section 8) shows the same pattern: it separates positives from random negatives (AUC 0.73) but not from hard negatives (AUC 0.52). Machine learning also allows measurement: precision@k, NDCG, and bias tests can be computed for a trained model and compared against keyword baselines.

Job-seeker flow.  The user uploads a resume, and the system extracts its text, skills and years of experience. The candidates are only postings that are still open and have an apply link: the open-jobs pool (Section 6). The bi-encoder retrieves the closest postings from this pool, and the reranker orders them. For each of the top three, the user sees the posting details, closing date, apply link, ATS score and skill gap. Postings past their closing date are never shown.

Role of external APIs.  Pretrained LLM APIs are used to (i) pre-label CV–job pairs and generate synthetic CVs for training data, (ii) act as a zero-shot baseline in the evaluation, and (iii) produce user-facing explanations and rewrites. The retrieval (bi-encoder [6]), reranking (cross-encoder [7]) and skill-extraction models are our own fine-tuned models. Every API call is logged.

Success criteria.  Job recommendation: precision@3 and NDCG@10 on the human gold set, compared with BM25/TF-IDF and zero-shot LLM baselines; candidate ranking is evaluated the same way. ATS score: rank correlation with the human fit labels (gold set and the external No / Potential / Good Fit pairs), and better than the baseline score in Section 8. Skill extraction: span-level F1 against the ESCO dictionary baseline. Bias: change in score under counterfactual swaps of name, gender, and university should be statistically indistinguishable from zero.

Evidence of collection.  Each source has a script in data_scripts/: download_kaggle.py (official Kaggle API when credentials exist, otherwise byte-identical public mirrors on the Hugging Face Hub), download_esco.py, scrape_jobs.py, generate_synthetic_cvs.py, and label_pairs.py. A download manifest records file names, sizes, row counts and origin. Every LLM call goes through llm_client.py, which appends the prompt, model, response, token usage and timestamp to logs/api_calls.jsonl and caches responses so no call is paid for twice. The client works with any OpenAI-compatible endpoint (OpenAI, Groq, Gemini).

Scraping policy.  The scraper downloads and obeys robots.txt for every URL, waits 2–3 s between requests (adaptive), sends a descriptive user agent, doubles its delay on HTTP 403/429, and stores only public posting text (title, company, city, description, skills tags, career level, experience). It reads structured data from the schema.org JobPosting block on each page rather than brittle HTML selectors. Postings were collected for 19 of 33 planned search queries (software, data, design, finance, banking, marketing, HR and sales). When the site began rate-limiting (87 of 555 requests answered HTTP 403), the scraper backed off and, after five consecutive blocks, stopped as the policy requires, so the current Pakistani sample is tech-heavy. On a refresh attempt on 2026-10-24, Rozee.pk answered the request for robots.txt itself with HTTP 403. The scraper treats this as the whole site being disallowed and made no further requests; we do not work around blocks (for example, by changing the user agent). All requests are logged to logs/scrape_rozee.log. Data is used for academic purposes only.

Open-jobs pool.  Users are only matched against postings they can still apply to. live_pool.py keeps the Rozee.pk postings that have an apply URL and whose closing date has not passed, and stores them with their skills, required years and embedding. LinkedIn postings closed in 2023–24 and are excluded automatically by the same date check; they remain training data. On 2026-10-24 the pool holds 348 open postings, but they close quickly: 340 close within seven days (median 4.1 days left), and 49% are IT roles. The pool therefore has to be refreshed every few days (make refresh scrapes new postings, then reruns cleaning, features and the pool). Because Rozee.pk currently blocks our crawler, Section 12 lists the alternatives we are pursuing.

Gold set.  sample_gold_set.py draws 200 pairs only from the test split (50% positives, 30% hard negatives, 20% random negatives; pair type hidden from annotators). Two members label them independently using written guidelines, and the script reports Cohen's κ (plain and linearly weighted) and flags disagreements for adjudication. This set is never used for training.

8Feature Strategy

Implemented in data_scripts/build_features.py and skills.py. All fitted transformers (scaler, encoder, TF-IDF vocabulary) are fitted on the training split only.

  • Text normalization: lowercasing, punctuation cleanup and light rule-based lemmatization for the TF-IDF baseline only; transformer inputs keep original casing and punctuation.
  • Text representations: TF-IDF over unigrams and bigrams (50,000 features, sublinear TF) as the baseline, and 384-dimensional L2-normalized sentence embeddings from all-MiniLM-L6-v2 [6], to be fine-tuned in Phase 3.
  • Skill extraction and normalization: a gazetteer of ESCO preferred and alternative labels plus curated tech synonyms maps variants (“JS”, “ReactJS”, “k8s”) to one canonical skill. Ambiguous single words are excluded. This yields on average 7.95 skills per resume and 3.91 per posting, and is the baseline the Phase 3 NER model must beat.
  • Encoding: resume categories and posting categories (posting titles mapped to the same 24 categories by ordered rules, covering 60.9% of postings) are label-encoded in a shared label space; skills are multi-hot encoded (MultiLabelBinarizer) over a vocabulary of 1,168 ESCO skills seen in at least five training documents.
  • Numeric features: text length, number of skills, and years of experience. Years are extracted by regex from explicit mentions (“5+ years of experience”). For postings, Rozee.pk's structured experience field (“3 Years – 5 Years”) is the fallback; for resumes, the span of dated job entries is, with “Current” anchored to the collection year of the source (2019) rather than today (coverage: 95.8% of resumes, 46.9% of postings). Heavy-tailed counts are log-transformed, missing years get a train-median value plus a missing indicator, and all are standardized with StandardScaler.
  • Pair construction: for each resume, positives are the 3 most similar postings of the same category; hard negatives are the 2 most similar postings of a different category; one random different-category posting is added. This gives 15,755 pairs (7,859 positive, 5,264 hard negative, 2,632 random negative), balanced 50/50.
  • Splits: 70/15/15 train/validation/test split by resume and by posting (not by pair), stratified by category, and pairs are only formed within a split, so no resume or posting leaks across splits. Resumes: 1,842 / 395 / 395. Postings: 19,661 / 4,213 / 4,214. Pairs: 11,052 / 2,351 / 2,352.
  • Per-job ATS score (baseline): ats_score.py scores one resume against one posting from 0 to 100 as a weighted mean of four components: skill coverage (share of the posting's ESCO skills found in the resume, weight 0.50), experience fit (resume years divided by required years, capped at 1, weight 0.20), title match (share of the posting title's content words found in the resume, 0.15) and resume structure (contact details, experience, education and skills sections present, 0.15). A component the posting does not specify, such as required years, is left out and the remaining weights are rescaled. The missing skills form the skill-gap report. On the 2,352 test pairs the mean score is 44.7 for positives, 43.3 for hard negatives and 31.4 for random negatives. Phase 3 replaces the skill component with the NER model and learns the weights from the gold set.

9Exploratory Data Analysis

All charts are produced by data_scripts/eda.py on the cleaned data.

Further observations.  (1) The Kaggle resumes are written by experienced professionals (median well above ten years of experience), while our primary users are fresh graduates; synthetic entry-level CVs and the Pakistani postings address this domain gap. (2) The LinkedIn postings are US-based. The Rozee.pk data adds local titles, cities and skill tags; its most requested skills are artificial intelligence, Python, computer science, CI/CD, Git. (3) 8 of the 348 Rozee.pk postings state an explicit gender preference (7 male, 1 female). We keep this field for the Phase 4 bias analysis and never use it as a model input.
